The week of January 20, 2025 — the first week of the new Trump administration — saw a coordinated set of executive actions and legislative proposals targeting the structural foundations of federal civil service protections. The most consequential action was [Executive Order 14171—Restoring Accountability to Policy-Influencing Positions Within the Federal Workforce](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/DCPD-202500138), which reinstates the Schedule F classification (renamed "Schedule Policy/Career"), reclassifying career federal employees in "policy-influencing" positions into the excepted service. The order's Section 3 introduces an explicit requirement that employees "faithfully implement administration policies," with failure constituting grounds for dismissal — potentially creating a political loyalty test for career civil servants. This was accompanied by a [Memorandum on Restoring Accountability for Career Senior Executives](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/DCPD-202500149), which reinterprets career Senior Executive Service positions as serving "at the pleasure of the President" and directs agency heads to "immediately take all appropriate actions, up to and including removal" of SES officials whose performance is deemed inconsistent with presidential priorities.

This convergence of executive and legislative action may indicate a systemic effort that could affect the merit-based civil service protections established by the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883 and reinforced by subsequent statutes. This might matter because, if implemented, these measures could convert thousands of career positions from merit-protected tenure to at-will employment contingent on political alignment, potentially undermining the institutional independence that insulates the federal workforce from patronage-based dismissals.

On the legislative side, two bills introduced on January 23 reinforce the executive actions. The [End the Deep State Act (HB697)](https://legiscan.com/US/bill/HB697/2025) would codify Schedule F into statute, removing it from the domain of executive discretion that allowed the Biden administration to rescind the original 2020 order. The [MERIT Act of 2025 (HB687)](https://legiscan.com/US/bill/HB687/2025) creates an "alternative removal" pathway for federal employees, amending Title 5 due process protections. Together with the executive actions, these represent a multi-branch strategy: executive orders for immediate implementation and legislation for permanence.

The President characterized the firings as "very common" and analogous to replacing U.S. Attorneys. Fifth, the IG dismissals, while legally questionable in method, do not necessarily preclude replacement with qualified, independent officials — the long-term impact depends on who is appointed and whether Senate confirmation processes function normally.
