The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) published two proposed rules in recent weeks that would significantly change how federal employees are evaluated and who gets laid off when agencies cut staff. Together, these proposals have raised questions about whether longtime protections against politically motivated firings could be affected.

The first rule, titled _"Reduction in Force"_ (March 5, 2026), would change the formula used when agencies decide which employees to let go during layoffs. Currently, seniority plays a major role — employees with longer service records are generally retained. Under the proposed change, performance ratings would take priority over tenure. While rewarding strong performers sounds reasonable, this shift matters because performance ratings are inherently more subjective than years of service, and could be subject to greater variability depending on how they are administered.

The second rule, _"Performance Appraisal for General Schedule, Prevailing Rate, and Certain Other Employees"_ (February 24, 2026), changes how those performance ratings work. It would allow agencies to use forced ranking systems (where a set percentage of employees must receive low scores regardless of actual performance), remove employees' ability to formally challenge their ratings through grievance procedures, and eliminate the requirement that the lowest ratings be reviewed for fairness.

The concern is about how these two rules interact. If performance ratings become subject to greater variability and are harder to challenge, and those same ratings then determine who keeps their job during layoffs, the combined effect may increase the risk of politically motivated removals. The traditional civil service system was specifically designed to prevent this — to ensure that career government workers could not be fired for their political views or for refusing to carry out improper orders.

There are alternative explanations worth considering. The administration has framed these changes as common-sense efficiency reforms that reward merit and improve accountability. Many private companies and some foreign governments use similar performance-based systems without documented political misuse. The administration has pointed to expected gains in workforce performance as a key rationale for the changes.

However, the timing and combination of these reforms — reducing accountability in ratings while simultaneously making ratings the primary factor in layoffs — warrants public attention. Both rules are currently in proposed form and open for public comment, meaning citizens and affected employees have an opportunity to weigh in before they take effect.
